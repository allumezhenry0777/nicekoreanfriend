Within a day of landing you'll notice them: a glowing GS25, CU, 7-Eleven or Emart24 on seemingly every block, open at every hour. Koreans treat convenience stores as an extension of their kitchen, wallet and junk drawer — and once you learn the moves, you will too.

## The short version

Convenience stores solve five travel problems at once: **food right now**, **cash right now** (ATM), **transit** ([T-money cards](/getting-around/t-money-card-guide) sold and topped up), **forgot-my-stuff** (chargers, socks, umbrellas, toiletries) and **it's 3 a.m. and everything else is closed**.

## How it actually works

**The food is real food.** Beyond snacks, there's a wall of fresh items restocked daily: lunchboxes (도시락, *dosirak*) with rice and several sides, triangle kimbap (삼각김밥) around a thousand-something won, sandwiches, salads, fruit cups and a serious dessert section.

**The equipment is free.** Every store has a microwave (the cashier or a counter sign will point you to it), a hot-water dispenser for instant ramyeon, chopsticks and spoons by the counter, and usually a table or window bench. The full ritual: buy cup ramyeon → fill at the hot-water machine → wait three minutes at the window counter → add a triangle kimbap. Total cost: pocket change. Satisfaction: disproportionate.

**Triangle kimbap has a technique.** Follow the numbers printed on the wrapper: pull tab 1 straight around, then pull corners 2 and 3 sideways. The seaweed wraps the rice as the plastic leaves. Get it wrong once like everyone else, then never again.

**The 1+1 wall is a game.** Tags reading **1+1** or **2+1** mean buy one (or two), get one free — Korea's beloved convenience-store sport. The free item must be the same product; grab both at the register, not later.

## What it costs

Rough anchors so you can sanity-check: triangle kimbap and small snacks sit in the ₩1,000–2,500 zone, cup ramyeon similar, lunchboxes in the ₩4,000–6,500 range, drinks from about ₩1,000. Prices are fixed and marked — no haggling, no tipping, no surprises.

## Common misconceptions

- **"It's junk food only."** Lunchboxes are balanced enough that office workers live on them; there's fruit, salads, boiled eggs and protein snacks.
- **"The cashier will chat with me."** Interactions are minimal and script-free: greeting, total, payment, done. "봉투 드릴까요?" means "do you want a bag?" — bags cost a small fee by law, so say "네" (yes) or "아니요" (no).
- **"ATMs inside take every card."** Many do take international cards (look for a "Global ATM" sticker or English menu), but fees and acceptance vary by machine — details in our [ATM guide](/money-banking/korea-atm-guide).

## What locals do

Use them constantly and unromantically: morning banana-milk-and-kimbap, midnight ramyeon after a night out, topping up T-money on the way to the subway, buying a clear umbrella the second it rains (it's the same umbrella everyone has, for a reason). Convenience-store tables are also a licensed people-watching venue — a beer at the window counter is a legitimate Seoul evening.

## Good to know

- Trash at the in-store bins is **sorted** — follow the pictures (liquids out first, cups here, plastic there). It's good practice for [Korea's recycling culture](/living-in-korea/korea-trash-recycling-guide).
- Stores near offices sell surprisingly decent coffee machines' brew for a fraction of cafe prices.
- Package pickup/drop-off services at CU and GS25 counters are a life upgrade for residents — see [receiving mail in Korea](/living-in-korea/korea-mail-packages-guide).
