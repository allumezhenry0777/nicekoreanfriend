Nobody expects you to arrive knowing how Korea works — locals are genuinely forgiving with visitors. But a handful of the same small mistakes trip up almost everyone on day one, and they're all easy to skip once someone tells you. Consider this that someone. None of these are about being "polite enough" to impress anyone; they're about not stranding yourself, annoying a train car, or paying more than you needed to.

## How we chose

These aren't obscure faux pas — they're the mistakes we watch newcomers actually make in their first 24 hours: the cash surprise, the escalator shuffle, the maps app that won't route. We prioritized things with a real consequence (a stuck payment, a fine, a missed train) or that quietly mark you as brand-new, and we paired each with a fix you can apply the same day. Prices reflect 2025–2026.

## The list

{/* IMAGE — inline: a labeled comparison of Naver Map vs Google Maps showing transit directions in Seoul · file: /images/articles/korea-first-day-mistakes-2.webp */}

### 1. Assuming card covers everything (no cash)

Korea is deeply card-friendly, so it's tempting to land with zero won. Then a traditional-market stall, a tiny noodle shop, or a T-money top-up wants **cash**, and you're stuck. Keep **₩50,000–100,000** on you. Pull it from a **Global ATM** (fee about **₩3,000–3,600**). More in [cash vs card in Korea](/money-banking/cash-vs-card-korea).

### 2. Relying on Google Maps for directions

Google Maps barely does driving and walking directions in Korea — a data-export restriction, not a bug you can fix. Download **Naver Map** or **KakaoMap** before you need them; both have English and nail subway, bus, and walking routes. They're the first thing on our [essential apps](/first-72-hours/essential-apps-korea) list.

### 3. Tipping

There's no tipping culture here. Restaurants, taxis, cafes — the listed price is the final price. A tip doesn't read as generous so much as confusing, and staff may chase you to return your "forgotten" change. Just say thank you and go.

### 4. Standing on the left of the escalator

On escalators, people **stand on the right** and leave the left side clear for anyone walking up. Block the left during rush hour and you'll feel the polite-but-pointed pressure of a dozen commuters behind you. Small thing, instantly noticed.

### 5. Hunting for a street trash can (and dropping litter when you can't find one)

Public bins are genuinely rare, and waste is sorted strictly into general, recycling, and **food waste** — improper disposal can even carry fines. The move locals make: carry your trash in your bag until you reach a bin at your accommodation, a cafe, or a convenience store. Our [trash and recycling guide](/living-in-korea/korea-trash-recycling-guide) has the full system.

### 6. Being loud on the subway

Trains are strikingly quiet. Loud phone calls, speakerphone video, or an animated chat with friends will turn heads. Keep calls short, use earphones (at a reasonable volume — Seoul's public-etiquette campaign now calls that out too), and leave the priority seats at each car's ends for the elderly, pregnant riders, and people with disabilities. See [Seoul subway basics](/getting-around/seoul-subway-guide).

### 7. Not getting a T-money card on day one

Fumbling for exact change or a fresh ticket at every ride wastes your whole first day. A **T-money card** (about **₩2,500–5,000**, loaded with cash at any convenience store) taps you onto subways, buses, and even taxis, and gives you cheaper transfers between them. Set it up first thing — [here's how](/getting-around/t-money-card-guide).

## Honorable mentions

- **Jaywalking.** Koreans wait for the green signal even on an empty street, and police do occasionally ticket. Just wait; you'll blend right in.
- **Wearing shoes indoors.** Many guesthouses, some restaurants, and all homes are shoes-off. If you see a step up and a shoe rack, that's your cue.
- **Writing names in red ink.** Traditionally associated with death — harmless but avoidable if you're filling out a form.
- **Skipping both hands.** When you receive change, a business card, or a drink from someone older, two hands (or a hand on your forearm) reads as respectful.
- **Blowing your nose at the table.** Considered rude during a meal; excuse yourself to the restroom instead.

## How to use this list

Don't try to memorize all of this as rules — you'll relax into most of it by watching what people around you do, which is honestly the best strategy in any new country. Fix the two with real consequences first: **carry cash** and **download a Korean maps app** before you leave the airport. The etiquette pieces (escalators, quiet trains, no tipping) you'll absorb within a day just by paying attention. And if you slip up? A quick "죄송합니다 (joesonghamnida — sorry)" and a smile smooths over basically anything. For the deeper dive, our [Korean etiquette basics](/culture-etiquette/korean-etiquette-basics) covers the rest.
