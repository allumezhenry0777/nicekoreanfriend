Korean food has a reputation for heat, and it's half-true — but the cuisine is really a spectrum from gentle, savory comfort food to industrial-grade chili. This list walks the spectrum in order, so you can calibrate as you go.

## How we chose

Three filters: available at ordinary restaurants everywhere (not just food-tour spots), friendly to a first-timer's palate somewhere on the list, and each one teaches you something about how Korean food works.

## The list

### The gentle openers (no chili involved)

**1. Bulgogi (불고기)** — thin beef in a sweet soy-garlic marinade, grilled or pan-cooked. The universal gateway dish; if a picky eater exists in your group, bulgogi is the answer.

**2. Kimbap (김밥)** — rice, vegetables, egg and ham rolled in seaweed, sold everywhere from ₩-cheap kimbap shops to [convenience stores](/food-dining/korean-convenience-store-guide). Picnic food, train food, budget-saver.

**3. Samgyetang (삼계탕)** — a whole young chicken stuffed with rice and ginseng in clear broth. Koreans eat it *hot in midsummer* to fight heat with heat; you can eat it whenever you need a hug.

**4. Japchae (잡채)** — sweet-potato glass noodles stir-fried with vegetables and a little meat, sesame-fragrant and mild. Often a side at feasts; also a main at home-style restaurants.

**5. Kalguksu (칼국수)** — hand-cut wheat noodles in anchovy or chicken broth. What locals eat on rainy days.

### The adjustable middle

**6. Bibimbap (비빔밥)** — the famous mix-everything rice bowl: vegetables, egg, often beef, and gochujang (chili paste) **served on the side or on top for you to dose yourself**. Add a little, mix violently (this is correct technique), taste, repeat.

**7. Samgyeopsal (삼겹살)** — thick pork belly you grill at the table, then wrap in lettuce with garlic, ssamjang (a savory, barely-spicy paste) and whatever else looks good. Not spicy unless you make it so; maximally social. Ordering logistics are in our [restaurant guide](/food-dining/how-to-order-korean-restaurant).

**8. Dakgalbi (닭갈비)** — chicken, cabbage, rice cakes and sweet chili paste stir-fried on a communal pan. Warm heat rather than pain; the fried-rice finale made in the leftover sauce is mandatory.

### Basic training complete

**9. Kimchi jjigae (김치찌개)** — the national comfort stew: aged kimchi, pork, tofu, bubbling in a stone pot. Medium heat with deep sour-savory flavor; the dish that converts kimchi skeptics.

**10. Sundubu jjigae (순두부찌개)** — silken tofu stew, usually with seafood or pork and an egg cracked in at the table. Adjustable at many places ("순한맛" = mild).

**11. Tteokbokki (떡볶이)** — chewy rice cakes in glossy red sauce; street-food royalty. Sweeter than it looks but genuinely spicy. Buy a small cup from a street cart first, not a full restaurant portion.

**12. Naengmyeon (냉면)** — buckwheat noodles in icy broth (물냉면, mild) or chili sauce (비빔냉면, not mild). Bizarre on paper, addictive in a Korean summer, traditionally the closing course after BBQ.

## Honorable mentions

Fried chicken and beer (치맥, *chimaek*) as a cultural institution; hotteok (호떡), the molten brown-sugar pancake of winter street stalls; gamjatang, the pork-spine potato stew rewarding the brave.

## How to use this list

Eat downward. Days 1–2: dishes 1–5. Days 3–4: the adjustable middle. After that, order kimchi jjigae like you've lived here for years. Screenshot the Korean names — pointing at them works in any restaurant in the country.
