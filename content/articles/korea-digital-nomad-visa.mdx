For years, remote workers in Korea lived in a gray zone: technically tourists, quietly working from Seongsu cafés, doing visa runs to Fukuoka. The **F-1-D workation visa** — launched as a pilot in January 2024 and made a permanent program in 2026 — finally gives remote employees of foreign companies a legal, renewable way to live here. Here's how it actually works, including the 2026 changes that made it reachable for a lot more people.

## What the F-1-D is (and isn't)

The F-1-D lets you **live in Korea while working remotely for a foreign employer or foreign clients**. It is explicitly *not* a work visa: you cannot take a job with a Korean company, freelance for Korean clients, or run a business here. Korea gets your rent and your café spending; the Korean labor market stays off-limits.

The initial grant is **one year**, extendable at a local immigration office. Under the permanent program the **maximum total stay is now three years**, up from two under the pilot — one of the quiet-but-significant 2026 upgrades.

## The income requirement — and the 2026 easing

This is the gate most people ask about first. The standard requirement is an annual income of at least **twice Korea's GNI per capita**. GNI resets every year; with the 2025 figure at about ₩52.4 million, the standard bar sits around **₩105 million (~US$75,000)** for 2026 applications.

The 2026 permanent program introduced eased tiers that are worth reading twice:

- **Aged 18–34, or planning to live outside the Seoul capital region** (Seoul/Incheon/Gyeonggi): the requirement drops on a sliding scale between one and two times GNI.
- **Both young and outside the capital region**: you can qualify at **one times GNI — roughly ₩52 million (~US$37,000)**, half the standard bar.
- **Designated population-declining regions**: further reductions apply.

If Seoul rents were already pushing you toward Busan or Jeonju, the visa rules now literally pay you to follow that instinct. Exact tier mechanics are published through immigration notices, so confirm your bracket on [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) or with your embassy before you build plans around it.

## The other requirements

Income aside, you'll need to show:

- **At least one year with your current employer** (or an equivalent track record of foreign-client income for the self-employed), proven with an employment verification letter issued recently.
- **Income evidence**: recent pay stubs, about three months of bank statements, and tax returns.
- **An apostilled criminal record check** from your home country.
- **Private health insurance** covering medical treatment *and repatriation* for your entire stay — commonly quoted at a minimum of around €70,000–₩100 million in coverage. Korea's National Health Insurance doesn't cover F-1-D holders the way it covers workers and students, so this private policy is genuinely what you'd rely on; our guide to [seeing a doctor in Korea](/health-safety/seeing-a-doctor-in-korea) explains what care costs without NHIS.
- A passport, photos, and the application forms.

You must be at least 18. Processing typically runs **three to four weeks**, with no paid fast-track.

## How to apply

There are two routes:

**1. From abroad**: apply at the Korean embassy or consulate in your country. Cleanest option if your paperwork is ready — you land with your status sorted.

**2. From inside Korea**: if you're already here visa-free or on a short-term stay, you can apply to **change status at a local immigration office** — a flexibility that was formalized as the program matured, and which spares you flying home just to file papers. Book the immigration office visit through HiKorea (reservations fill fast in Seoul; see our [HiKorea walkthrough](/work-study-visas/extend-visa-korea-hikorea) for how the booking system works).

Either way, the sequence is the same: gather documents (start with the criminal check and apostille — slowest part), get the employer letter issued fresh, buy the insurance policy, then file.

## After approval: setting up your life

Once you're in on the F-1-D, you're a registered long-term resident, which means the standard settling-in pipeline applies: get your [residence card (ARC)](/living-in-korea/arc-residence-card-guide) within 90 days, then use it for a [Korean bank account](/money-banking/open-bank-account-korea) and a [proper phone plan](/phone-internet/korea-phone-plan-residents) — the ARC is what unlocks [Korean identity verification](/phone-internet/korean-identity-verification-guide), without which half of Korean app life stays locked.

For daily money, remember your salary lands in a foreign account: a Wise-style multi-currency card plus a Korean account for rent and utilities is the setup most nomads here converge on. And test your home setup honestly — Korean apartment internet is famously fast, but your video calls will live at 1 a.m. if your team is in New York. Choose your neighborhood for the [subway line](/getting-around/seoul-subway-guide) *and* the time zone math.

## Is it worth it?

If you earn above the threshold and your company blesses the arrangement, the F-1-D is one of the best remote-work deals in Asia: world-class internet, safety, food, and now up to three years of it without visa runs. The income bar is real, but the 2026 regional easing changed the math — a 28-year-old developer on $40k who's happy in Daejeon now qualifies where they couldn't in 2025. Check the current GNI figures, count your documents, and give yourself two months of lead time.
