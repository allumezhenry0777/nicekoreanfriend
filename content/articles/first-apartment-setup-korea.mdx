You've got the keys and an empty (or half-empty) room, and suddenly you need everything at once. Good news: Korea makes this cheap and fast if you go in the right order. Here's the exact move-in checklist I give every friend — tasks first, then the shopping — so you're sleeping comfortably by night one.

## How we chose

This isn't a "nice to have" wish list. Everything here is either legally required, something that stops working if you ignore it, or a genuinely cheap fix for a real move-in headache. I've prioritized the order things bite: paperwork and utilities first (they take days to sort), then the Daiso-level essentials you can grab in an afternoon, then furniture you can add over your first week. Prices are 2025–2026 real-world figures for a single person in a small officetel or one-room, so your mileage varies by city and building.

## The list

### 1. Register your move at the district office (전입신고)

Within 14 days of moving in, go to your local 주민센터 (jumin-senteo — community center) and file a 전입신고 (jeonipsingo — move-in report). It's free, takes minutes, and — crucially for renters — pairs with a 확정일자 (hwakjeong-ilja — date stamp) to legally protect your deposit. It's also part of the address trail you'll need for your Residence Card. Details on the deposit side are in our [renting in Korea guide](/living-in-korea/renting-in-korea-explained).

### 2. Turn on electricity, gas and water

Electricity (KEPCO) usually carries over, and you just call to put the account in your name — expect roughly ₩15,000–30,000 a month in spring and autumn, more like ₩40,000–60,000 when the AC or electric heat is running. **Gas is the one that traps people:** the city gas company almost always needs an in-person inspection appointment before they'll switch it on, so book it a few days ahead, especially in winter, when heating bills run around ₩50,000–120,000 a month. Water is often bundled into your building's maintenance fee.

### 3. Get internet installed

Pick a provider (KT, SK Broadband or LG U+) and book an install. A standalone 1Gbps plan runs roughly ₩33,000–44,000 a month, and cheaper 100Mbps deals start around ₩22,000 on a longer contract; install fees are often waived by promotion. Many officetels have internet pre-wired or included in maintenance fees, so ask before you sign anything.

### 4. Buy your city's 종량제 trash bags

You can't legally throw out general trash without official **종량제 (jongnyangje — volume-rate) bags**, sold at any convenience store or supermarket. Bag color is city-specific and not interchangeable. Grab a small food-waste bin too. The full rules — and the fines for getting it wrong — are in our [trash and recycling guide](/living-in-korea/korea-trash-recycling-guide).

### 5. The Daiso run: cleaning and organizing

Nothing at Daiso tops ₩5,000, so this is your cheapest fast start. Get sponges, rubber gloves, dish soap, a bathroom squeegee, a lint roller, hangers, a laundry drying rack (or clips), a power strip or two, and a small trash bin plus a food-waste caddy. A single ₩30,000–50,000 basket covers most of a bare room.

### 6. Bedding and sleep

If your place came with a bed frame or a raised floor, you still need a mattress topper or 요 (yo — floor mattress), a pillow, a duvet and sheets. Daiso and marts cover the cheap end; for a proper set, a mart or IKEA runs ₩50,000–150,000. This is the one thing worth not skimping on for night one.

### 7. Kitchen basics

A pot, a frying pan, a couple of plates and bowls, chopsticks and a spoon, a knife and a cutting board, and dish soap. Daiso plus a mart handles it for well under ₩50,000. Add a cheap rice cooker (₩30,000–60,000) if rice is your staple.

### 8. Furniture, the cheap way

Before buying new, check **당근마켓 (Danggeun-maket — Karrot)**, Korea's hyper-local secondhand app, where people in your exact neighborhood sell desks, chairs, shelves and drying racks for a fraction of retail, often free if you carry it away. IKEA and marts fill the gaps. Set it up over your first week, not your first day. Karrot is one of the apps we cover in our [essential apps guide](/first-72-hours/essential-apps-korea).

## Honorable mentions

- **A small dehumidifier or moisture boxes** — Korean summers are humid, and closets mildew fast.
- **A folding drying rack** — most people air-dry laundry indoors; dryers are still uncommon.
- **Indoor slippers** — you take shoes off at the door, and floors get cold in winter.
- **A basic screwdriver and command hooks** — for assembling secondhand finds and hanging things without damaging walls.
- **A doorway or under-sink organizer** — small Korean rooms live or die by vertical storage.

## How to use this list

Work top to bottom on day one for items 1–4 (the paperwork and utilities that take real time), then knock out 5–7 in a single afternoon of shopping. Furniture can wait a week while you learn what the room actually needs. Set up your bank account and phone early, since utility and internet contracts want a Korean account and number — start with [opening a bank account](/money-banking/open-bank-account-korea). Do it in this order and you'll go from bare room to real home for a few hundred thousand won, not a few million.
