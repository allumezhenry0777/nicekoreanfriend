Korean restaurants can feel like everyone else got a manual you didn't: people summon staff with buttons, water appears from a machine in the corner, and the bill gets paid standing up by the door. Here's the manual.

## Before you start

Two mindset shifts. First, **service is on-demand**: staff come when called, which is a feature, not neglect. Second, **banchan (반찬) — the small side dishes that arrive unasked — are free**, shared by the table, and usually refillable. They're not an appetizer you'll be charged for.

## What you need

Nothing but an appetite. Menus at busy places increasingly have photos, English, or both; when they don't, Papago's camera mode ([get it here](/first-72-hours/essential-apps-korea)) reads menus fine, and pointing at another table's food is a time-honored, completely acceptable technique.

## Step-by-step

1. **Enter and read the room.** If there's a host, they'll ask "몇 분이세요?" (how many?) — hold up fingers. If nobody greets you at a casual place, seat yourself at any open table.
2. **Find the ordering method.** In rough order of frequency: a **tablet on the table** (look for an English toggle, usually a flag or "ENG" button), a **call bell** on the table's edge or wall, or the classic shout — "저기요!" (*jeogiyo*).
3. **Order.** Dish name plus quantity is plenty: "비빔밥 두 개 주세요" (*bibimbap du gae juseyo* — two bibimbap, please). Pointing at the menu with "이거 주세요" (*igeo juseyo* — this, please) works flawlessly.
4. **Get your own water.** Look for the water station (정수기): metal cups, cold water, self-serve. Cutlery lives in a box on the table or in a drawer built into the table's side — check the drawer; it delights everyone the first time.
5. **Eat, refill, repeat.** Want more of a side dish? Bell + "반찬 좀 더 주세요" (*banchan jom deo juseyo*). Free, expected, no eye-rolls.
6. **Pay at the counter.** In most casual places the bill lives on your table or is tracked by table number, and you pay standing at the register by the exit. Cards are accepted essentially everywhere. **No tipping** — there's no tip line, and leaving cash causes confusion, not gratitude.

## What it costs

Everyday solo meals (soups, rice bowls, noodle dishes) cluster around the several-thousand-won range that makes Korea one of the best value food countries anywhere; BBQ and shared feasts scale with meat and drinks. Prices are on the menu, tax included — what's printed is what you pay.

## Common problems (and fixes)

- **The tablet is Korean-only.** Look for 🇬🇧/ENG in a corner; failing that, photo-translate it, or press the bell and order the old way.
- **A dish arrived that I didn't order.** It's almost certainly a free side or a service item ("서비스" — on the house). Enjoy it.
- **I'm eating alone — is that weird?** Solo dining (혼밥) is mainstream, and counter seating exists for exactly this. Some BBQ places want 2+ portions; the menu will say.

## Good to know

- At Korean BBQ, staff often cook or start the meat for you, and scissors-and-tongs are the standard tools. Let them lead; jump in when they hand things over.
