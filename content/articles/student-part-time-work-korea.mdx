Here's the sentence that saves you from the most common visa mistake international students make in Korea: **your student visa does not include the right to work — a separate permit does, and it has to be approved before your first shift.** Korean immigration polices this more seriously than most students expect, and the price of getting it wrong is paid at your next [visa extension](/work-study-visas/extend-visa-korea-hikorea), exactly when you can least afford problems.

The system is called **시간제취업허가 (siganje chwieop heoga)** — the part-time work permit. Here's how it works as of late 2026.

## Who can apply, and when

**D-2 degree students** (bachelor's through doctorate) are eligible once enrolled — most universities also require you to have completed your first semester and to be in decent academic standing. **D-4 language students** have a waiting period: you generally become eligible only **after six months** of study in Korea, with attendance requirements attached. Fresh arrivals at [language institutes](/work-study-visas/korean-language-schools-guide) planning to fund term two with café work need to budget for that gap.

Exchange students' eligibility depends on their program; ask your international office before assuming anything.

## The hour limits — and why TOPIK is money

The weekly ceilings depend on your program level *and* your proven Korean ability, and the gap is big enough to plan your life around:

- **D-2 undergraduates** who meet the Korean-language benchmark for their year (TOPIK or KIIP levels — the required level scales as you advance): generally up to **25 hours per week on weekdays**, with weekends and official vacation periods treated more generously.
- **Graduate students** meeting the benchmark: a higher weekday ceiling, commonly up to 30 hours.
- **Anyone below the language benchmark**: the cap drops sharply — to roughly **10 hours a week** for undergraduates. Same visa, same tuition, less than half the earning ceiling.

Two students in the same class can therefore have completely different legal work lives, purely on TOPIK scores. If part-time income matters to your budget, treat the TOPIK fee as an investment with a triple return: work hours now, [F-2-7 residency points](/work-study-visas/korean-visa-types-explained) later, and better job applications always.

The exact benchmark tables get tweaked periodically, so confirm your specific combination on [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) or with your international office rather than a classmate's memory of last year's rules.

## What jobs are (and aren't) allowed

The permitted list covers the jobs students actually want: **cafés, restaurants, convenience stores, retail, office and administrative work, translation assistance, and research assistant roles** connected to your studies.

The prohibited list is where people stumble:

- **Nightlife and adult-entertainment venues** — categorically off-limits.
- **Delivery riding, parcel work and designated-driver (대리운전) gigs** — restricted, despite being the most advertised flexible work in Korea.
- **Manufacturing/factory work** — generally restricted, with exceptions tied to high Korean proficiency.
- **Private cash tutoring** — the quiet trap; see the FAQ below.

And the structural rule: the permit **names your specific employer**. Working anywhere not on your permit — including a second job you didn't add — counts as unauthorized work even if your hours are under the cap.

## How to apply

The order of operations trips people up, so: **job first, permit second, work third.**

1. **Get hired** (conditionally). You need the employer's details — business registration, workplace address, your role and hours — for the application. Decent employers in student-heavy neighborhoods know this dance well; an employer who shrugs at the permit is telling you something about how they'll handle your pay, too.
2. **University sign-off.** Your international student office confirms your enrollment and standing — this is also where attendance problems surface.
3. **File the application** with the employer's documents, your enrollment and language-score evidence — via HiKorea's e-application or at your immigration office.
4. **Wait for approval, then start.** Not before. The permit runs alongside your sojourn period, and changing workplaces means updating it.

Once you're working, you'll want a [Korean bank account](/money-banking/open-bank-account-korea) for wages, and it's worth knowing that legal student workers are covered by Korea's minimum wage — check the current rate, as it rises most years, and count your paid breaks. Employers who pay cash "to keep it simple" are usually keeping something else simple, too.

## What happens if you skip all this

Unauthorized work is one of the most commonly caught student violations — surfacing through workplace inspections, tax records, tip-offs, and extension reviews. Consequences scale from fines through denied extensions to, for repeat or flagrant cases, cancelled status and departure orders. The permit process, by contrast, costs a few documents and a couple of weeks.

Korea genuinely wants students to be able to work — the system has loosened over the years, not tightened. It just insists on the paperwork.
