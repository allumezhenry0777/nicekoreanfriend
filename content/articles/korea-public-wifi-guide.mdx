One of the small joys of Korea is how connected it feels. You step onto a subway train, and there is free WiFi. You sit in a tiny cafe, and there is free WiFi. Even the traditional markets and the bus have it. As someone who grew up here, I honestly forget it is unusual — until a visitor gasps that the train has better signal than their hometown. Here is how to use it well, and where to be a little careful.

## The short version

Korea blankets its public spaces with free WiFi run by the city governments and the big telecom carriers. The networks you will see most are **Public WiFi Free** (open, no password) and **Public WiFi Secure** (encrypted, a little safer). In Seoul you will also spot **Seoul_Free_WiFi** and **Public WiFi@Seoul**, and carrier zones like **T WiFi Zone**, **KT_Free_WiFi**, and **U+Zone**. All are free. Use them happily for maps and browsing; just be thoughtful before typing a password or card number on an open one.

## How it actually works

The government and carriers install access points in high-traffic places and let anyone hop on. You will find working WiFi on subway trains and platforms, city buses and bus stops, inside libraries, community centers, traditional markets (시장 — sijang), government buildings, and of course cafes and fast-food chains.

Connecting takes a few taps:

1. Open your WiFi settings and pick an official network, like **Public WiFi Free** or **Seoul_Free_WiFi**.
2. A login page (captive portal) should pop up on its own. If it does not, open a browser and visit any site — naver.com works — to force it.
3. Choose your language, tick the terms box, and tap connect or **동의 (dongui — agree)**.
4. You will see a confirmation, and you are online.

The encrypted **Public WiFi Secure** takes a one-time setup to install a security profile, but after that it reconnects automatically and is safer than the open version. It is worth doing once if you will be in Korea a while.

## What it costs

Nothing. That is the headline. These networks are publicly funded or carrier-provided and free to use, with no time limit and no payment. Cafe WiFi is free with your purchase — you will sometimes find the password printed on the receipt or a sign by the counter. The only "cost" is the security trade-off on open networks, which we will get to.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Free WiFi means it is safe."** Not quite. An open network is convenient, but a shared open connection is a weaker barrier against snooping. That does not make Korea dangerous; it makes basic caution smart, the same as anywhere.
- **"I do not need mobile data if WiFi is everywhere."** Coverage is wide but not total — you will hit dead spots between hotspots, and speeds crawl when a train car is packed. A [SIM or eSIM](/phone-internet/esim-vs-sim-vs-pocket-wifi-korea) is still worth having as your always-on layer.
- **"All the networks are the same."** The encrypted "Secure" versions genuinely protect your traffic better than the plain "Free" ones. When there is a choice, pick Secure.

## What locals do

Honestly? We connect without thinking about it, but we have quiet habits. For anything sensitive — mobile banking, logging into accounts, entering a card or passport number for a booking — most people either switch to their own mobile data for those two minutes or make sure they are on an encrypted network. We stick to sites that show the padlock (https). And plenty of longer-term residents keep a VPN on their phone for exactly these moments. None of this is paranoia; it is just the digital version of not flashing your wallet in a crowd.

## Good to know

A few practical tips. Save the **Public WiFi Secure** setup once and let it auto-connect afterward — it is the best mix of free and safe. If a network keeps failing to load its login page, forget it in settings and rejoin fresh. And remember that WiFi is a companion to mobile data, not a replacement: for a smooth first day you want both, which is why sorting out an [eSIM](/phone-internet/korea-esim-setup-guide) or [prepaid SIM](/phone-internet/korea-prepaid-sim-guide) still matters. Once you are connected either way, load up the [essential apps for Korea](/first-72-hours/essential-apps-korea) — with maps, messaging, and translation ready, this astonishingly well-wired country opens right up.
