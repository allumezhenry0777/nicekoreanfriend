The Seoul subway carries millions of people a day, runs on time, has spotless stations, heated seats in winter, phone signal everywhere — and full English signage. It is genuinely hard to get lost. But there's a difference between surviving it and riding it like you live here. This is the second one.

## The short version

Get a [T-money card](/getting-around/t-money-card-guide), tap in, follow the color of your line, and learn to love exit numbers. That's 90% of it. The rest is etiquette and a few local habits.

## How it actually works

**Lines are numbered and color-coded.** Line 2 (green) is the famous circle line that hits many major neighborhoods. Signs show both the line color/number and the direction — direction is indicated by the *final station* on that branch, so know the end-of-line name for your direction (e.g., "towards Sindorim").

**Every exit has a number, and Koreans navigate by them.** When a cafe says "Hongik Univ. Station, Exit 9," that's a precise instruction: leave through Exit 9 and the directions start from there. Naver Map tells you which exit to use for any destination — trust it, because big stations can have a dozen exits hundreds of meters apart.

**Transfers are corridors, not new tickets.** Changing lines happens inside the gates: follow the colored signs for your next line through the transfer passage. Some transfers are long walks; that's normal. Your fare continues — no re-tapping inside the paid area.

**Tap out matters.** Fares are distance-based on top of the ₩1,550 base (with a transit card, as of 2026), calculated when you exit. The gate screen shows what you paid and your remaining balance.

## What it costs

- Base fare **₩1,550** with T-money, **₩1,650** as a single-ride ticket — plus small distance surcharges on longer trips.
- Transfers between subway lines are free inside the gates; bus↔subway transfers within 30 minutes are discounted rather than charged as a new trip.
- Seoul also sells an unlimited monthly pass (the "Climate Card") — only worth it for residents commuting daily, not for a short visit.

## Common misconceptions

- **"I need the paper subway map."** The network is huge; the map is a beautiful bowl of spaghetti. Use the app — Naver Map or Kakao Metro plans routes, picks the fastest transfer, and shows real-time arrivals and the best car to board for a quick transfer.
- **"Rush hour is like Tokyo."** It's crowded (7:30–9 a.m., 6–7:30 p.m., and Line 2/9 especially) but there are no pushers, and off-peak trains are comfortable.
- **"Priority seats are fine if they're empty."** The seats at car ends marked for elderly/pregnant/disabled passengers are left empty by many locals even in packed trains. As a visitor, the safe move is: don't sit there.

## What locals do

- Stand on the **right** on escalators (walking on the left happens even though it's officially discouraged).
- Line up at the **floor markings** beside the doors and let people off first — this one is taken seriously.
- Keep phone calls short and quiet; the subway is surprisingly hushed. Eating a meal on board is a no; a discreet sip of coffee is tolerated on the subway (buses are stricter).
- Grab a bakery snack *after* exiting instead — every major station is attached to an underground mall.

## Good to know

- Station names are announced in Korean, English, and often Japanese and Chinese. Transfer stations get a distinctive jingle — you'll learn to react to it.
- Toilets are clean, free and inside or just outside the gates at nearly every station.
- Phone reception and 4G/5G work in tunnels, so you can navigate mid-ride.
- Lockers ("Happy Box" and similar) at major stations solve the "luggage before check-in" problem.
