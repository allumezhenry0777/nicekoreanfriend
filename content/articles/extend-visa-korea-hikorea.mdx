Every long-term resident of Korea eventually plays this game: your sojourn period is expiring, the immigration office's reservation calendar looks like a sold-out concert, and a form asks you questions in vocabulary no language class taught. Here's the whole process, in order, with the traps marked.

First, a mindset shift that saves people real pain: what you extend is not your *visa* but your **period of sojourn (체류기간, ch'eryu gigan)** — the length of stay attached to your status inside Korea. It's printed on your [ARC](/living-in-korea/arc-residence-card-guide) paperwork and it, not the visa sticker in your passport, is the date that matters.

## Step 1: Find your date and start a month early

Look up your expiry on your ARC or on [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) (My Page → sojourn status). Applications are accepted well in advance — generally up to four months before expiry — and one to two months early is the comfortable window. Waiting until the final week is how people end up refreshing the reservation page at midnight.

Mark the date somewhere loud. Immigration does not send you a reminder.

## Step 2: Check if you can skip the office entirely (e-application)

HiKorea's **e-application (전자민원)** lets many statuses — students extending with the same school, workers extending with the same employer, and other straightforward same-conditions renewals — apply entirely online: upload documents, pay the fee, receive the result electronically. No queue, no day off work.

On HiKorea: **E-application → Extension of sojourn period**, log in (foreigners register with passport and ARC details), and the system tells you whether your status and situation are eligible. If your circumstances changed — new school, new employer, new visa category — you'll usually be routed to an in-person visit instead.

One quirk to expect: HiKorea's English interface is real but partial, and payment steps may lean on Korean identity-verification flows. Our [identity verification guide](/phone-internet/korean-identity-verification-guide) explains the phone-based verification that makes Korean websites cooperate.

## Step 3: If you need to go in person — book the visit reservation

In-person applications require a **visit reservation (방문예약)** made through HiKorea; walk-ins without one are turned away at most offices for extension matters. Choose your jurisdiction office (it's assigned by your registered address — Seoul residents mostly go to Seoul Immigration Office in Mok-dong; Sejongno and regional offices cover their own districts), pick a date and time slot, and print or save the confirmation.

Slots at big-city offices genuinely run out one to three weeks ahead, and evaporate fastest around February and August when every student in the country is renewing. This is the single most common reason people accidentally cut it close — the appointment shortage, not the paperwork.

## Step 4: Gather documents

The core set is stable across statuses:

- Passport and ARC
- The application form (downloadable from HiKorea, or at the office)
- Fee payment — extensions cost roughly **₩30,000–60,000** depending on type, paid by revenue stamp or card
- Proof of your address, such as a lease — see [how Korean leases work](/living-in-korea/renting-in-korea-explained)
- Proof you still qualify for your status: enrollment certificate and transcript for students, employment certificate and business registration for workers, bank statements where financial capacity applies

Then each status adds its own garnish — attendance records for D-4 language students, income documents for some E visas. HiKorea lists the exact set per status; bring more than you think you need, because the marginal cost of an unused photocopy is zero and the cost of a missing one is a second appointment.

## Step 5: The appointment itself

Arrive early, take a number matching your reservation, and expect the office to feel like a very international DMV. The officer reviews your file, may ask short questions about your plans, and has real discretion — polite, organized applicants with tidy documents get the smooth version. Straightforward cases are often decided the same day, with the new sojourn period recorded on the spot or sent for processing; complex ones get a follow-up date.

The whole visit typically runs one to three hours depending on the office's mood. Bring your phone charger and something to read.

## What not to do

**Don't overstay — at all.** A single day is a recorded violation with a fine; weeks or months bring bigger fines and possible entry bans. If it's already happened, self-report at an immigration office before traveling; voluntary declaration is consistently treated better than being flagged at departure.

**Don't work outside your status while extending.** An extension review is exactly when unauthorized work surfaces — students, this is why the [part-time work permit](/work-study-visas/student-part-time-work-korea) exists.

**Don't trust old blog posts on fees and forms** — including, in principle, this one. We verify against HiKorea and re-date our guides, but the reservation system's details shift. The pattern, though — check e-eligibility, book early, over-prepare documents — has been stable for years, and it's the pattern that keeps the process boring. Boring is the goal.
