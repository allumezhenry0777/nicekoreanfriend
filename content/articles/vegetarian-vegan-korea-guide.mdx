Korea has a reputation as a hard place to eat vegetarian, and it's half deserved. The half that's true: animal ingredients hide in dishes that look completely plant-based. The half that's wrong: with a couple of phrases and a sense of where to look, you can eat wonderfully here — this is, after all, the country that perfected an entire vegan cuisine in its Buddhist temples 1,500 years before anyone coined the word. Let's make it easy.

## The short version

The single most important thing to understand is **hidden broth and fish products**. A bowl of vegetable soup, a spoonful of kimchi, a plain-looking stew — any of them can carry animal ingredients you'd never see. Learn to ask about the stock, know which dishes are usually safe, and use **temple food (사찰음식 — sachal eumsik)** and apps as your reliable fallback. In Seoul especially, dedicated vegan restaurants now sit every few blocks in areas like Itaewon and Hongdae.

## How it actually works

Three ingredients cause almost all the trouble:

- **Anchovy stock (멸치육수 — myeolchi-yuksu).** The default base for a huge share of soups and stews, including many that look purely vegetable. Even doenjang jjigae usually starts here.
- **Fish sauce (액젓 — aekjeot) and shrimp paste (새우젓 — saeu-jeot).** Standard in most kimchi and many banchan (side dishes). That innocent red kimchi on the table is very often not vegan.
- **Beef or pork base.** Bits of meat or a meat broth turn up in things like gimbap (ham), bibimbap (beef, egg), and fried rice.

So your job isn't to memorize a huge list — it's to **ask two questions** and know a handful of safe defaults.

### Dishes that are usually safe (confirm the broth)

- **비빔밥 (bibimbap)** — order it without meat and egg; the rice, greens, and gochujang carry it.
- **잡채 (japchae)** — glass noodles with vegetables (sometimes cooked in a little meat; ask).
- **김밥 (gimbap)** — ask for it without ham and egg (야채김밥, yachae-gimbap, is the veggie version).
- **파전 / 감자전 (pajeon / gamja-jeon)** — savory scallion or potato pancakes.
- **순두부찌개 (sundubu-jjigae)** — soft tofu stew, but **check the broth**; many use anchovy or beef.
- **사찰음식 (temple food)** — the safest category of all, below.

## What it costs

Everyday veg-friendly eating is cheap: a **김밥 (gimbap)** roll runs a few thousand won, and a plate of **비빔밥 (bibimbap)** or **잡채 (japchae)** typically lands around **₩8,000–13,000**. Dedicated vegan cafes and restaurants in Seoul sit a bit higher, roughly **₩12,000–20,000** for a main. The splurge is a **temple-food course**: a multi-course set at a refined restaurant like the Michelin-listed **Balwoo Gongyang** near **Jogyesa Temple** runs well into the tens of thousands of won per person — worth it once for the experience. Convenience stores are a budget backstop; see our [convenience store guide](/food-dining/korean-convenience-store-guide).

## Common misconceptions

- **"Kimchi is just vegetables, so it's vegan."** Usually not — most kimchi contains fish sauce or shrimp paste. Ask, or seek out temple-style kimchi.
- **"If there's no visible meat, it's safe."** The broth is the trap. A clear or vegetable-looking soup is frequently built on anchovy stock.
- **"Vegetarian and vegan are understood the same way."** Not always. "채식 (chaesik)" can include people who still eat some seafood or eggs, so being specific — especially about fish and broth — matters.
- **"Temple food is bland."** The opposite. It's layered, seasonal, and celebrated worldwide precisely because it coaxes deep flavor without meat, garlic, or onion.

## What locals do

**Temple food (사찰음식)** is the local secret weapon: Buddhist cuisine that excludes all meat, fish, dairy, and eggs, plus the "five pungent vegetables" (garlic, onion, scallion, leek, chives). It's effectively vegan by design. You'll find it around **Insadong** and **Jogyesa Temple**, and some temples offer templestay meals.

For finding places, veg-savvy locals and long-termers live on **HappyCow**, which has **300-plus listings in Seoul** (accuracy varies, so read recent reviews). Neighborhood-wise: **Itaewon and Haebangchon (HBC)** have the widest range and the most international options; **Hongdae** skews toward young vegan cafes and health spots; **Insadong** leans traditional and temple-food-friendly. When ordering in a regular restaurant, they simply state their needs clearly up front rather than mid-meal.

## Good to know

Keep these phrases in your pocket:

- **고기 빼주세요 (gogi ppae-juseyo)** — please leave out the meat.
- **저는 채식해요 (jeoneun chaesik-haeyo)** — I'm vegetarian.
- **완전 비건이에요 (wanjeon bigeon-ieyo)** — I'm fully vegan.
- **육수가 뭐예요? (yuksu-ga mwoyeyo?)** — what's the stock/broth?
- **멸치나 해산물 들어가요? (myeolchi-na haesanmul deureogayo?)** — does it contain anchovy or seafood?
