South Korea is generally a comfortable destination for visitors, but "safe" should never mean switching off your normal judgment. This guide separates the everyday risks worth planning for from the dramatic headlines that often shape expectations before a trip.

## The short version

The current U.S. travel advisory for South Korea is **Level 1: exercise normal precautions**, and UK travel guidance says crime against foreigners is rare. That is reassuring, not a guarantee. Protect your passport and valuables, keep drinks in sight, use licensed transport at night, avoid demonstrations, and pay close attention to traffic and severe-weather alerts.

## How it actually works

**Why it often feels safe.** Public transport is widely used into the evening, many commercial streets remain active late, and police and emergency services are easy to contact. You may see locals leave a phone or bag at a cafe table, but visitors should still keep passports, wallets and devices with them.

**Crime is uncommon, not absent.** Official travel guidance notes occasional theft and cases of assault, including sexual assault, particularly around bars and nightlife areas. Keep bags closed in crowds, watch your drink, use legitimate taxis or public transport, and call **112** if you are threatened or a crime occurs.

**The real hazards are unglamorous:**

- **Traffic.** Delivery motorbikes use sidewalks and crosswalks with commitment; cars turn right through crossings. Look both ways even on a green man, and give scooters right of way they legally don't have but will take anyway.
- **Weather.** Heavy rain and flooding can occur during the summer rainy season, usually in June and July, while the broader typhoon season can extend from June to November. Follow Korea Meteorological Administration forecasts and government emergency alerts; English alerts are available through the National Disaster and Safety Portal and the Emergency Ready app.
- **Alcohol.** Korean drinking culture is warm, generous and cumulative. Soju's sweetness disguises its efficiency. Pace yourself; every convenience store sells hangover remedies for a reason.
- **Hiking.** Korea's mountains are beautiful, extremely accessible and occasionally underestimated — proper shoes and daylight margins for anything beyond a neighborhood trail.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Tension with North Korea never affects visitors."** Daily life usually continues normally, but tensions and civil-emergency instructions can change with little notice. Monitor official travel advice and follow local authorities rather than relying on social-media commentary.
- **"Scams target tourists everywhere."** Organized tourist scams are rare. The closest equivalents: a minority of street-market stalls quoting flexible prices (fixed-price shops dominate anyway) and bar tabs in expat nightlife zones deserving a glance before paying.
- **"Safe country = safe everything."** Korea's excellent safety stats coexist with real issues locals discuss — hidden-camera crime, nightlife drink-safety — which is why local caution norms (drink in sight, official taxis late) are worth copying rather than dismissing.

## What locals do

Locals commonly use public transport late, check real-time rain and fine-dust alerts, and choose licensed taxis or a taxi app when trains stop running. Copy the practical habits—especially watching for turning vehicles and scooters—without copying the habit of leaving valuables unattended. ## Good to know

Save the numbers before you need them: **112** for police, **119** for fire or ambulance, and **1330** for multilingual tourist information and interpretation. They are explained in our [emergency numbers guide](/health-safety/korea-emergency-numbers).
