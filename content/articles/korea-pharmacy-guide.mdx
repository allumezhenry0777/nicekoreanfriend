Getting a cold your first week in Korea is almost a rite of passage, and the good news is that fixing it is easy and cheap. The Korean pharmacy — 약국 (yakguk — pharmacy) — is where you'll get most everyday medicine, and unlike back home, you don't grab it off a shelf yourself. You talk to a pharmacist who hands you what you need. Here's how to do it without stress, even with zero Korean.

## Before you start

First, know the difference between a pharmacy and a convenience store. A **pharmacy (약국)** is staffed by a licensed pharmacist and sells real medicine — cold remedies, painkillers, allergy pills, stomach aids, and anything from a doctor's prescription. A **convenience store** (CU, GS25, 7-Eleven, Emart24) can only sell a fixed government list of 13 "safety" items — a couple of Tylenol types, two cold medicines, four digestives, and two pain patches. That's your 3 a.m. backup, not your first choice.

Spotting a pharmacy is simple: look for a **green cross** sign and the characters 약국. In cities they're everywhere — near subway exits, inside or beside clinics, in any busy shopping street. If you've just landed and are still finding your feet, our [essential apps guide](/first-72-hours/essential-apps-korea) covers the map apps that'll point you to the nearest one.

One thing that trips people up: **antibiotics are prescription-only** in Korea. So is pseudoephedrine (the decongestant in Sudafed). If you need those, you'll see a doctor first — see our guide to [seeing a doctor in Korea](/health-safety/seeing-a-doctor-in-korea).

## What you need

{/* IMAGE — inline: a phone showing the Papago translation app with a Korean symptom phrase, held up next to a pharmacy counter · file: /images/articles/korea-pharmacy-guide-2.webp */}

Almost nothing. For over-the-counter medicine you don't need ID or a prescription — just walk in.

- **Your phone** with a translation app (Papago or Google Translate) if you're nervous about words.
- **Cash or a card** — both are fine; card is accepted almost everywhere.
- **For filling a prescription:** the printed prescription (처방전 — cheobangjeon) your doctor gives you. Some pharmacists may ask for your passport or Alien Registration Card, so keep your [ARC](/living-in-korea/arc-residence-card-guide) handy if you have one.

You do **not** need to speak Korean. Pharmacists deal with pointing, gestures, and phone screens all day. A few phrases help, and our [phrases for travelers](/culture-etiquette/korean-phrases-for-travelers) guide has more.

## Step-by-step

**1. Walk in and get the pharmacist's attention.** No ticket or line number for OTC — just approach the counter.

**2. Describe your symptom, not a brand.** Koreans buy medicine by symptom. Say or show: "감기 (gamgi — cold)," "두통 (dutong — headache)," "콧물 (konmul — runny nose)," "설사 (seolsa — diarrhea)," "목 아파요 (mok apayo — my throat hurts)." The pharmacist chooses the right product.

**3. Answer a quick follow-up.** They may ask about fever, allergies, or whether it's for an adult. A nod, a headshake, or a translated sentence is plenty.

**4. Pay and listen for the dosage.** They'll tell you how many to take and how often — usually "하루 세 번 (haru se beon — three times a day), 식후 (sikhu — after meals)." If unsure, ask them to type the schedule into your translator.

**For a prescription:** hand over the 처방전, wait a few minutes while they package the pills (often into neat little dose packets), pay your share, and go. With [national health insurance](/health-safety/korean-health-insurance-explained), your out-of-pocket for common medicine is usually just a few thousand won.

## What it costs

Everyday medicine in Korea is genuinely affordable:

- **Pain and fever relievers:** about ₩3,000–8,000.
- **Cold medicine:** about ₩5,000–15,000.
- **Digestive aids / antacids:** about ₩4,000–10,000.
- **Prescription medicine (with insurance):** often just ₩1,500–5,000 for a common course.

Prices are set per product, so they don't vary much shop to shop. Note one quirk: outside normal hours — after about 6 p.m. on weekdays, after 1 p.m. on Saturdays, and on Sundays and holidays — a small night surcharge (roughly 30% added to the dispensing fee) can apply.

## Common problems (and fixes)

**"Everything's closed."** Most pharmacies shut around 6–8 p.m. on weekdays and 1–2 p.m. on Saturdays, and many close Sundays entirely. Fix: search "야간약국 (night pharmacy)" or "24시간 약국 (24-hour pharmacy)" on Naver Map or Kakao Map, or use the HIRA (건강보험심사평가원) pharmacy finder. Seoul runs dozens of public late-night pharmacies open until around 1 a.m.

**"It's the middle of the night."** Head to any 24-hour convenience store for the basic 13-item list — Tylenol, a cold liquid, digestives, and pain patches. Enough to get you to morning. Our [convenience store guide](/food-dining/korean-convenience-store-guide) shows what else they stock.

**"I can't read the box."** Take a photo and run it through Papago's image translation, or just let the pharmacist choose — that's the whole point of the counter model here.

**"The dose feels strong."** Korean Tylenol (타이레놀 — Tylenol) often starts at 500mg tablets rather than the 325mg you may know. Read the box and don't double up out of habit.

## Good to know

- **Familiar brands exist.** You'll spot 타이레놀 (Tylenol) and 지르텍 (Zyrtec, for allergies) on the shelves. Korean go-to painkillers include 펜잘 (Penzal) and 게보린 (Gevorin).
- **Pharmacists are a real first stop.** For a minor cold, sore throat, or upset stomach, many people ask the pharmacist before ever seeing a doctor — it's fast, cheap, and no appointment needed.
- **Keep the packet.** Prescription pills come in labeled dose packets; keep the paper slip in case you need to show what you're taking.
- **When it's more than minor,** don't tough it out. If symptoms are severe or lasting, see a doctor — and for anything that feels like an emergency, know your [emergency numbers](/health-safety/korea-emergency-numbers) (119 for an ambulance). Korea is a very [safe and easy place to get care](/health-safety/is-korea-safe); the system just works a little differently.
