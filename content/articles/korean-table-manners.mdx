Korean meals are social machinery: shared dishes, shared grill, shared bottle. The manners exist to keep that machinery friendly, and they're less scary than the internet suggests. Here's the honest rulebook, sorted by how much each rule actually matters.

## The short version

Chopsticks never stand upright in rice. Rice bowl stays on the table. Elders start first. Pour for others, not yourself, ideally with two hands. Congratulations — you now outperform most first-time visitors.

## How it actually works

**The spoon is your friend.** Korean dining uses a chopsticks-plus-spoon set (수저, *sujeo*). The spoon handles rice and soup — lifting the bowl to your mouth, normal in Japan or China, is the thing *not* done here. Bowl on table, spoon to mouth. Chopsticks handle everything else; using both at once in one hand is the only real foul.

**Chopstick red lines.** Upright in rice: never (it mimics funeral incense). Beyond that: don't spear food like a harpoon, don't dig through a shared dish for the best piece, and rest chopsticks on the bowl's edge or the table, not across your rice.

**Shared dishes are actually shared.** Banchan and stews are communal; taking from them with your own chopsticks is normal among friends and family. In formal company, use serving spoons where provided. Double-dipping into shared sauce after biting: same rule as everywhere on earth.

**Elders anchor the meal.** At a shared table, the eldest lifts their spoon first and, ideally, sets it down last-ish — don't sprint ahead or bolt early. In practice someone will wave you to start ("먼저 드세요" — please go ahead); accept graciously.

**The drinking ritual is the real etiquette exam — and the most fun.** Never pour your own glass; fill others' glasses when they're empty and someone will fill yours. Receive a pour by holding your glass with two hands (or one hand with the other touching your arm); pour for elders the same way. First toast is communal — "건배!" (*geonbae*). With notably senior company, the traditional extra credit is turning slightly away as you sip. Soju mechanics, chimaek culture and how to pace yourself deserve their own guide — the short version is: eat while you drink, Koreans always do.

**Sounds and signals.** Slurping noodles: mildly acceptable, less enthusiastic than Japan. Blowing your nose at the table: step away instead. Talking with mildly full mouth mid-banchan-debate: happens at every Korean table, don't stress.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Finishing every grain is mandatory" / "leaving food is mandatory."** Neither. Eat what you can; leaving some banchan untouched is completely fine — they're a spread, not a challenge.
- **"You must use metal chopsticks perfectly."** Korean flat metal chopsticks are objectively harder than wooden ones — locals know this and find visitor struggles endearing. Forks exist. Pride is optional.
- **"Paying is split at the table."** Historically the eldest or the inviter pays, and friendly fights over the bill are a national sport; younger groups increasingly split via app. As a guest, offer once sincerely, lose gracefully, and reciprocate with coffee after — that two-step is textbook Korean.

## What locals do

Watch any table of Koreans: spoons in soup, chopsticks flying over shared dishes, glasses refilled the moment they empty, "잘 먹겠습니다" (*jal meokgesseumnida* — "I'll eat well," said before eating) and "잘 먹었습니다" (*jal meogeosseumnida* — "I ate well," said after). Those two phrases, plus the pouring ritual, are 90% of looking like you belong.
