Korea has some of the fastest, most affordable healthcare in the world — but the first time you need it, the system feels unfamiliar. There's no gatekeeping family doctor, no week-long wait for an appointment. You just walk in. Here's exactly how to see a doctor here, even without a word of Korean.

## Before you start

The single most useful thing to understand is **where to go**. Korea's clinics come in tiers:

- **의원 (uiwon — clinic):** a small neighborhood practice, one or two doctors. This is your default for colds, flu, stomach trouble, skin problems, minor injuries, and prescriptions. Walk-in, cheapest, everywhere.
- **병원 (byeongwon — hospital):** larger, with more departments and beds, for things a clinic refers on.
- **종합병원 (jonghap byeongwon — general hospital):** the big ones, for specialists, surgery, serious tests, and emergencies.

Clinics are usually specialized by a sign out front — 내과 (naegwa — internal medicine) for general illness, 이비인후과 (ibiinhugwa — ENT) for throat/nose/ear, 정형외과 (jeonghyeongoegwa — orthopedics) for bones and joints, 피부과 (pibugwa — dermatology) for skin. For a cold, 내과 or ENT is a safe bet.

**When it's an emergency** — chest pain, serious injury, trouble breathing — skip all this and call **119** for an ambulance or go straight to a hospital ER (응급실 — eunggeupsil). Keep our [emergency numbers guide](/health-safety/korea-emergency-numbers) saved.

## What you need

- **ID:** your [Alien Registration Card](/living-in-korea/arc-residence-card-guide) if you're a resident, or your passport if you're visiting. Since 2024, clinics generally must verify your identity when you use insurance, so always carry it.
- **Payment:** a card is fine almost everywhere; bring some cash as backup.
- **A translation app:** Papago handles Korean best; Google Translate works too. See our [essential apps guide](/first-72-hours/essential-apps-korea).
- **Insurance status:** if you're enrolled in [national health insurance](/health-safety/korean-health-insurance-explained), the clinic looks you up automatically by your ID — you don't need to bring a card.

## Step-by-step

**1. Find a clinic.** Search the department name (e.g. "내과") plus your neighborhood on Naver Map or Kakao Map. The listing shows hours and reviews. Most clinics open around 9 a.m., often break for lunch (roughly 1–2 p.m.), and close around 6 p.m.

**2. Register at reception (접수 — jeopsu).** Hand over your ID. First-timers fill a short form — name, birthdate, contact, and the reason for the visit. If it's in Korean, use your translator or ask; staff are used to helping.

**3. Wait for your number.** You'll get a number and a seat. Waits at a clinic are typically short — often 10–30 minutes.

**4. See the doctor (진료 — jinryo).** The consultation is brief and focused, usually 5–15 minutes. Describe symptoms simply; show a translated sentence if needed. Don't be surprised if it's quick — that's normal here, not a brush-off.

**5. Pay on the way out.** Settle up at the reception desk. They'll hand you a prescription (처방전 — cheobangjeon) if you need one.

**6. Fill the prescription at a pharmacy.** There's almost always a 약국 (pharmacy) right next to or below the clinic. Hand over the slip, wait a few minutes, pay a few thousand won. Our [pharmacy guide](/health-safety/korea-pharmacy-guide) walks through that part.

## What it costs

Korea's prices are one of the nicest surprises for newcomers.

**With national health insurance**, you pay only your share — about 30% at a clinic. A standard visit often lands around **₩5,000–15,000**, with medicine adding another few thousand.

**Without insurance** (tourists, or before you're enrolled), you pay the full rate: roughly **₩30,000–80,000** for a clinic consultation, plus **₩5,000–15,000** for medicine. A hospital or specialist runs higher, and an ER visit can be ₩200,000 or more.

Because uninsured care adds up fast, short-term visitors should carry **travel medical insurance** — it's the main protection against a surprise bill, since [NHIS](/health-safety/korean-health-insurance-explained) only covers residents.

## Common problems (and fixes)

**"I can't find an English-speaking doctor."** The highest concentration is in Seoul's Gangnam area, and every major hospital — Severance, Samsung Medical Center, Asan, Seoul National University Hospital — has an **international healthcare center** with English-speaking staff and interpreters. For a simple clinic visit, though, a translation app is usually enough.

**"I'm stuck communicating."** Call **1345** (Immigration's free multilingual hotline) or **1339** (24-hour medical guidance) for interpretation and advice in English. You can also translate as you go on your phone.

**"The visit felt too fast."** That's normal. If you have several concerns, list them up front and ask, "다른 것도 봐 주세요 (dareun geotdo bwa juseyo — please check this too)."

**"Which clinic type do I pick?"** When unsure, start with 내과 (internal medicine). They'll refer you onward if it's outside their scope.

## Good to know

- **Walk-ins rule.** For everyday illness you rarely need an appointment — just show up during opening hours.
- **Bring your ID every time.** Identity checks at reception are now standard when you use insurance.
- **Weekends are thinner.** Many clinics close Sundays; a few open Saturday mornings. For weekend illness, hospital ERs and some larger clinics stay open.
- **Keep your paperwork.** Ask for a receipt and any records if you'll claim on travel or private insurance later.
- **You're in good hands.** Korea's care is fast, modern, and cheap by global standards — see why we call it one of the [easiest places to stay safe and well](/health-safety/is-korea-safe). The learning curve is just the system, not the quality.
