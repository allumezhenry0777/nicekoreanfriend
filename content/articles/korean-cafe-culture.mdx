Korea may be the most caffeinated society on earth by cafe density — multiple cafes per block in city neighborhoods, from megachains to shoebox roasteries to themed wonderlands. For visitors the cafe is also the easiest cultural on-ramp: low stakes, no language barrier that a menu board can't solve, and unlimited people-watching.

## The short version

Counter-order, counter-pay, take the buzzer, self-serve pickup, self-bus your tray. One drink per seat is the polite baseline. Stay as long as you like within reason. No tipping, ever.

## How it actually works

**Ordering.** Menus are posted above the counter, heavy on espresso drinks, ades (fruit sodas), teas and desserts. Many chains have English on kiosks — look for the language toggle. The two magic phrases: "아이스 아메리카노 주세요" (iced Americano, please) and "따뜻한 라떼 주세요" (hot latte, please). Sizes often run Tall-ish to enormous; "레귤러" (regular) is always safe.

**Hot or iced is a formal decision.** Every drink order triggers "아이스로 드릴까요, 따뜻한 걸로 드릴까요?" (iced or hot?). Answer "아이스" or "핫/따뜻한 거." Koreans overwhelmingly answer iced — see FAQ.

**The buzzer system.** After paying you receive a vibrating puck (진동벨). Sit anywhere; when it buzzes and flashes, collect your order at the pickup counter (픽업대). Return the buzzer at pickup. This system is universal, from Starbucks to hole-in-the-wall.

**Leaving.** Bus your own table: tray, cup and trash go to the return station (반납대), which has sorted bins — ice/liquid in one, cups in another, plastic lids and straws separate. Watch one local do it once; the choreography takes ten seconds.

**Dine-in means a real cup.** Regulations discourage disposable cups for dine-in, so drinking in means a mug or glass at many cafes, and asking for a to-go cup "for here" may be politely refused or come with a deposit-style workaround. If you might leave soon, just say "테이크아웃" (takeout) upfront.

## What it costs

An Americano at a mega-chain sits in the low-thousands of won; specialty roasters and dessert cafes climb from there, with signature desserts often costing more than the coffee. Rule of thumb: coffee in Korea costs like a light meal, and Koreans treat it as one of life's non-negotiable line items.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Camping with a laptop is rude."** It's a national institution. Reciprocate with periodic orders and reasonable space usage, and you're a citizen in good standing.
- **"Independent cafes are risky if I can't speak Korean."** Pointing at the menu plus "이거 주세요" works in a 6-seat roastery precisely as well as in a Starbucks. Independents are where the best coffee and interiors live — go.
- **"Cafes close late everywhere."** Big chains often run late; charming independents frequently close early evening. Naver Map hours are accurate — check before crossing town for a famous one.
- **"Dessert cafes are for groups."** Solo dessert (a slice of cake with your book) is fully normalized. This country invented entire cafe genres for being alone in public comfortably.

## What locals do

Cafe-hop as a hobby ("카페 투어"), photograph drinks before touching them (the drink is 50% visual media by design), claim tables with personal items *before* ordering (a trust culture flex that shocks visitors — see [Is Korea safe?](/health-safety/is-korea-safe)), and treat the third cafe of the day as normal. When in Seoul: pick a neighborhood famous for cafes — and simply walk until charmed. You cannot fail.

## Good to know

WiFi is fast and free essentially everywhere (password printed on the receipt or posted at the counter), power outlets are common at chains, and bathrooms often require a door code — also printed on the receipt.
