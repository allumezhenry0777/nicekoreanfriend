The Residence Card is the little plastic card that unlocks normal life in Korea. You'll hear people call it the ARC (Alien Registration Card) — that's the old name, and the official term is now 외국인등록증 (oegugin-deungnokjeung — Residence Card). Without it you can't open a proper bank account, get a phone contract, or sign a lease in your own name. Here's how to get one without the usual confusion.

## Before you start

The one rule that matters: if you're staying **longer than 90 days**, you must register with immigration and get a Residence Card **within 90 days of arriving**. This applies to most long-stay visas — student (D-2), work (E-series), spouse (F-6), and so on. Tourists on a short stay don't need one.

The whole system runs through **HiKorea** (hikorea.go.kr), the government's immigration portal. Since April 2021, immigration offices have required an online reservation — walk-ins are generally turned away. So your first job isn't gathering documents, it's grabbing an appointment, because slots in busy districts (Gangnam, Mapo, Jongno, or anywhere near a big university) can be booked out 1–4 weeks ahead.

You apply at the immigration office that covers **your address**, not whichever one is closest or most convenient. Check the jurisdiction on HiKorea before you book.

## What you need

Bring the originals, plus a photocopy of your passport photo page to be safe. The core set is:

- **Passport** (and a copy of the ID page).
- **One passport-style photo** — 3.5cm x 4.5cm, white background, taken within the last 6 months. There are photo booths near most immigration offices if you forget.
- **The application form** (통합신청서 — integrated application). You can fill it in at the office or download it from HiKorea beforehand.
- **Proof of your address** — usually your lease/rental contract (임대차계약서), or a dormitory certificate, or a confirmation of residence if you're staying with someone.
- **The fee** — ₩35,000, payable at the office. Many offices take card, but carry cash as backup.

Then there are **status-specific documents**: students bring a certificate of enrollment and tuition receipt; workers bring an employment contract and sometimes a business registration copy from the employer; F-6 spouses bring the marriage certificate and family papers. The exact list depends on your visa, so confirm yours on HiKorea's guide or ask your school's or company's international office — they do this constantly.

## Step-by-step

1. **Make a HiKorea account.** Go to hikorea.go.kr, switch to English, and register. You'll need your passport details.
2. **Book "Reserve Visit."** From the menu choose e-Application / Reserve Visit, pick the office for your district, and select a date and time. Save the confirmation.
3. **Gather your documents** from the list above. Double-check the visa-specific items the night before.
4. **Go to the office on time.** Take a queue number, hand over your documents at the counter, pay the ₩35,000 fee, and give your fingerprints if asked.
5. **Choose pickup or delivery.** You can return to collect the card, or pay about ₩3,000 extra to have it sent by registered mail to your address.
6. **Wait and track.** Processing is typically 2–4 weeks. You can check status on HiKorea or by calling **1345**, the immigration hotline (English available, no area code needed).

A nice recent upgrade: Korea now offers a **Mobile Residence Card** app, so once your card is issued you can carry a digital version on your phone for many everyday uses.

## What it costs

- **Government fee:** ₩35,000 for a new registration. This went up from ₩30,000 in January 2025 when Korea switched to the IC-chip card.
- **Mailing:** about ₩3,000 if you don't want to return in person.
- **Photo:** roughly ₩5,000–10,000 at a booth or photo shop if you need one taken.

That's it — no hidden charges. Reissuing a lost card later costs a similar fee, so treat the card carefully.

## Common problems (and fixes)

- **No appointments available.** Check HiKorea early in the morning when cancellations reappear, or look at a neighboring office within your jurisdiction. Some universities run group registration — ask your international office first.
- **Wrong office.** If you booked the office that doesn't cover your address, they may refuse you. Confirm jurisdiction before booking, not after.
- **Address proof rejected.** A handwritten note won't cut it. Bring a signed lease, a dormitory certificate, or an official confirmation of residence. If you live with a partner or friend, you may need their ID copy and a consent letter.
- **The 90-day clock.** If your appointment lands after day 90 purely because slots were full, book as early as you can and keep the confirmation as evidence of good faith. Don't leave it to the last week.
